# 002 — Atoms, Charge, and Electrons

## A Useful Model of the Atom

An atom contains a nucleus surrounded by electrons.

The nucleus contains:

- **protons**, which carry positive electric charge;
- **neutrons**, which carry no net electric charge.

Electrons carry negative electric charge.

For this project, we do not need a complete course in quantum mechanics. We need a practical model that explains why electrical behavior is possible.

## Charge Creates Interaction

Positive and negative charges attract each other. Charges with the same sign repel each other.

That means charged particles can exert forces without touching in the ordinary mechanical sense. When electrical conditions change in a material, electrons respond to the electric field created by those conditions.

This response is what allows electrical energy and signals to move through circuits.

## Electrons Are Not All Held Equally

Electrons occupy allowed energy states around the nucleus. The outermost electrons are called **valence electrons**.

Valence electrons matter because they participate most directly in chemical bonding and electrical conduction.

Different elements hold their valence electrons with different strengths. That helps explain why materials behave differently:

- In many conductors, some electrons can move relatively freely through the material.
- In insulators, electrons are held more tightly and do not move easily under ordinary conditions.
- In semiconductors, electron behavior can be controlled through material structure, temperature, electric fields, and added impurities.

## What Actually Moves in a Wire?

In a metal wire, the atomic nuclei remain arranged in the solid material. The mobile charge carriers are electrons.

It is tempting to imagine one electron racing from a battery through the entire circuit to a device. That picture is misleading. The electric field establishes itself through the circuit, and many electrons already present in the conductor begin drifting.

A useful analogy is a tube already filled with balls. Push one ball into one end and another may move out the far end quickly, even though no individual ball crossed the full tube instantly.

The analogy is imperfect, but it separates two ideas:

- the **signal or field effect** can propagate through the circuit rapidly;
- the average **drift speed** of individual electrons can be much slower.

## Charge Must Have a Path

Electric current requires a path through which charge carriers can move. A complete path is called a **closed circuit**.

If the path is broken, the circuit is open and sustained current cannot flow through the break.

This simple fact will later become computationally important. A transistor controls whether a conducting path is available. By controlling current, it can represent and manipulate logical states.

## From Physical Charge to Information

An electron is not a bit by itself. Information appears when a system assigns meaning to distinguishable physical states.

For example, a circuit might interpret:

- a low voltage range as `0`;
- a higher voltage range as `1`.

The exact values depend on the technology. The important point is that physical states become symbols because the circuit is designed to interpret them consistently.

This is the bridge from physics to information.

## What Comes Next

Charge explains what can move. The next chapter introduces the measurements used to describe electrical circuits:

- voltage;

- and power.

These ideas will let us describe how electrons are pushed, how much charge flows, and what opposes that flow.
